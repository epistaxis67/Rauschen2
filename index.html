// MANIFESTOthe discarded returns

Every system draws a line between what counts and what interferes. Whatever falls on the far side we call noise — and throw away. That discarding is not a fact of nature. It is a decision, and like any decision it can be reversed.

In The Parasite, Michel Serres showed that interference is not an accident of communication but its condition: the included third that is neither sender nor receiver and that, precisely thereby, produces the channel in the first place. No noise, no message to stand out against it. The parasite does not sit at the edge of the system — it sits at its center. And Friedrich Kittler turned this into a method: noise is the trace of the materiality of the medium, that which escapes meaning and the clean surface and is nonetheless physically there. Whoever reads only the message fails to hear what it is made of.

But to think interference merely as constitutive falls short. One has to give the discarded its full emphasis. Georges Bataille called l'informe, the formless, not a lack of form but a word that brings down — that undermines the very order demanding every thing keep its form. The formless "has no rights in any sense and gets crushed everywhere, like a spider." And in his general economy the accursed share, the part maudite, is that surplus no system can usefully recover — the expended, the thrown-away, sovereign not despite but in its uselessness. What a measurement discards as waste is Bataille's accursed part: what the ledger cannot absorb, and what counts for exactly that reason.

What a system discards as interference is not worthless.
It is the excluded — and it returns.

That it returns is Derrida's insight. No order erases its other completely; a remainder is left, a restance that will not dissolve, and the margin is not the incidental but what constitutes the center in the first place. Meaning itself arises from différance — the spacing, the deferral, the difference from which one signal separates from another. Hans-Jörg Rheinberger translated exactly this into science: the epistemic thing is at first the resistant, the indeterminate — noise, until an experimental system forms it into an object of knowledge. Julia Kristeva names it more bodily still, the abject: the cast-out that must be discarded for an order to form, and that keeps working at the border, uncanny and productive. And Georges Canguilhem, against every smooth norm, shows that the deviant, the pathological, is no mere defect but the source from which life creates new norms. Deviation is not error. It is the site where the new appears.

The history of the sciences is the history of this return. The hiss in Penzias and Wilson's antenna was the echo of the Big Bang. The trembling of pollen grains was the motion of atoms. The DNA fragments in the blood, taken for waste, now carry the diagnosis. Noise becomes signal — not because the data change, but because the model with which we listen changes. And today every person emits an unbroken stream of such discarded signals: the pulse waveform behind the heartbeat, the aperiodic floor of the EEG, the micro-tremor of the voice, the fine structure beneath every smoothed curve. Devices measure it and filter it away in the same breath. It lies fallow — the accursed, formless, abject other of the clean measurement.

But perhaps there is another case, one that lies differently from those three. György Buzsáki reasoned that in a system with 1/f dynamics, rare, extremely large events ought to be inevitable — and then observed that they never occur. A paper by Freyer, Ritter and Breakspear responds that the problem was not observing such events but forming an adequate null distribution for their absence. The events, then, were in the data; the instruments had long been good enough. What was missing was the model in which they could appear as something other than chance. Whether the case may be read this way is an open question — and one we intend to ask. Because if so, it is of a different kind than the three above: not a better instrument, but a better imagination.

With that, the real question shifts. In a present where data accrue in abundance and compute barely knows a limit, the bottleneck is no longer the technically feasible. The bottleneck is imagination. What stays noise stays noise not because it is unmeasurable, but because no one has conceived a model in which it counts. The discarded is the index of our imaginative limit — it marks exactly the line up to which we dare to conceive what a signal could be. And so the thinking must be inverted: not from the possible toward the actual, but from the impossible. For Derrida the im-possible is not what denies possibility but what first opens it — the coming that withdraws from calculability and thereby lets the new occur. This is where design fiction enters: it invents impossible instruments not as play but as a probe. A conceived device that reads out a noise filtered away today traces the border of the sayable — it is, with Isabelle Stengers and Donna Haraway, a speculative fabulation: the serious exercise of imagining that things could be otherwise, there, in the interstices of what presents itself as reality.

This is where the atlas begins. To hunt "gold in the data trash" does not mean mining a raw material others overlooked. It means recovering the excluded — giving the formless a form without domesticating it, and taking the impossible as the condition of the possible. Noise is not scarce, and compute is long past scarce. What is scarce is the nerve to conceive a model in which the discarded speaks — paired with the honesty to then test it. For not every remainder is a treasure. But every treasure was once a remainder no one dared to think.

// the thread between the cards

The System shows where the phenomenon holds signal potential at all. The Devices translate that to the practical level: which raw material actually accrues and where it is discarded. The Setups turn it into concrete protocols. The two contact cards name the people who help find — and test. The Questions are the guide that turns those people into co-searchers. The Literature gathers the voices that lead from noise to signal.

One principle ties it all together: whether something is "noise" is not a property of the data but a decision of the model — and the limit of that model is not technology but imagination. To use this atlas is to conceive a model in which the discarded speaks — and then to honestly test whether the gold is gold.
